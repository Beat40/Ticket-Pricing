SECTION 0 — DOMAIN CONTEXT

This section provides necessary background for readers unfamiliar with professional football (soccer) or the English Premier League structure.

0.1 What the English Premier League is

The English Premier League (EPL) is the highest level of the English football league system. It consists of 20 professional clubs. The league operates on a double round-robin format: every club plays every other club twice during a season — once at their own stadium (a "home" match) and once at the opponent's stadium (an "away" match). A full season comprises 38 matches per club (380 matches in total across the league), typically running from August to May.

0.2 How points work

Success in the league is measured by points. A club receives 3 points for a win, 1 point for a draw (both teams scoring the same number of goals), and 0 points for a loss. These points accumulate in a "League Table" or ranking that is updated after every match. At the end of the 38-match season, the final table determines the champion and various critical qualifications or demotions.

0.3 Why table position matters commercially

Table position is not just about prestige; it has massive financial implications. There are three primary "boundaries" in the table:

  • Position 1 (League Champion): Receives the highest share of prize money and global prestige.
  • Positions 1-4 (Champions League Qualification): The top four clubs qualify for the UEFA Champions League, a pan-European competition. This qualification is worth an estimated £100M+ in additional broadcast and commercial revenue.
  • Positions 5-7 (Europa/Conference League): These clubs qualify for secondary European competitions, which offer significant but lower revenue compared to the Champions League.
  • Positions 18-20 (Relegation): The bottom three clubs are "relegated" or demoted to the second division (the Championship). This is an existential threat for smaller clubs, resulting in a loss of over £100M in annual broadcast revenue, player departures, and reduced sponsorship.

0.4 Why match stakes are dynamic and not fixed

In a standard knockout tournament, a "Final" is always the most important match. However, in the EPL, the importance (or "stakes") of a match depends entirely on the current league table and the number of matches remaining. A match between Arsenal and Manchester City in August might have relatively low stakes, but the same fixture in April could be a "Title Decider" if both clubs are close in points at the top of the table. This system dynamically calculates these stakes to adjust pricing accordingly.

0.5 Home ground and pricing constraints

When a club plays at home, they control the ticketing for their stadium. However, they must provide a fixed allocation of approximately 3,000 seats (the "Away End") for fans of the visiting club. Crucially, the price for these away tickets is capped by league rules at £30. The home club is free to dynamically price all other areas of the stadium (the "Home End").

0.6 Season tickets and inventory constraints

Most EPL clubs sell "Season Tickets" before the season begins, granting fans access to all 19 home matches for a fixed upfront price. For "Big Six" clubs like Arsenal, over 85% of the stadium is often pre-sold to season ticket holders and members. This means dynamic pricing can only be applied to the remaining 3-15% of the seats, making the optimization problem one of managing a very limited and valuable "dynamic inventory."

SECTION 1 — SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE OVERVIEW

1.1 The business problem being solved

EPL clubs face a complex yield management problem. They often set ticket prices months in advance without knowing the eventual importance of a match. Static pricing models frequently leave revenue on the table during high-stakes matches (where demand far exceeds supply) and risk empty seats during low-stakes matches. This system provides an automated, data-driven pipeline to set optimal prices that reflect real-time demand signals.

1.2 The four-component pipeline

The system is built as a sequential data pipeline:

  • Conjoint Analysis Engine: Ingests synthetic fan survey data and uses Hierarchical Bayesian Multinomial Logit (HB-MNL) modeling to recover Willingness-to-Pay (WTP) in GBP. It outputs fan segments and price bounds for each club tier.
  • Dynamic Stakes Engine: Runs continuously, consuming live standings data. It produces a match_stakes_score (0.0 to 1.0) for every fixture, quantifying its competitive importance.
  • Demand Forecasting Engine: Combines time-series (STL, SARIMA) and tabular (LightGBM) models to predict P10/P50/P90 fill rates per seating zone. It takes match features as input and outputs a probabilistic demand forecast.
  • LP Optimizer: A Linear Programming solver that ingests the demand forecast and price bounds to recommend optimal zone prices. It enforces constraints like the £30 away cap and attendance floors for smaller clubs.

1.3 Distinction from previous systems

While sharing a lineage with the SHV handball pricing system, this EPL implementation adds five critical architectural components: a dynamic stakes engine based on league standings, an inventory segmentation layer for season-ticket-heavy venues, club-tier routing in the optimizer, three specialized conjoint models for different fan demographics, and five refined booking curve archetypes.

2.3 Dynamic Inventory Calculation

Dynamic inventory is the slice of seats actually available for pricing by the LP Optimizer. It is calculated as:

dynamic_inventory = capacity × dynamic_inventory_pct

Worked Example 1 (Arsenal): 60,704 × 0.03 = 1,821 seats. Despite the massive stadium, only 3% of seats are available to the general public outside the membership/season-ticket schemes.

Worked Example 2 (Bournemouth): 11,307 × 0.35 = 3,957 seats. Paradoxically, the smaller club has more dynamic inventory to manage because they have a lower proportion of pre-sold season tickets.

SECTION 3 — THE DYNAMIC STAKES ENGINE

3.1 Necessity for Dynamism

In a league format, no match is "High Stakes" at the start of the season. Match importance emerges as the table develops. A match in Matchweek 35 between 17th and 18th place is a "Relegation Six-Pointer" with massive stakes, but that same fixture in Matchweek 2 is just a "Standard" match. The StakesEngine uses live points, goal difference, and standings to quantify this urgency.

3.2 The Urgency Multiplier

Stakes matter significantly more as the end of the season approaches. We apply a linear urgency multiplier:

urgency = 0.1 + (matchweek - 1) * (0.9 / 37)
  • Matchweek 1: 0.10 (Early season, low urgency)
  • Matchweek 20: 0.56 (Mid-season, mounting pressure)
  • Matchweek 38: 1.00 (Maximum urgency)

The engine returns a dictionary containing:

  • match_stakes_score (float, 0.0-1.0): The final consolidated urgency score.
  • match_stakes_label (string): Descriptive category (Standard, High Stakes, etc.).
  • games_remaining (int): Matches left in the 38-game season.

SECTION 4 — CONJOINT ANALYSIS: THE THREE-TIER WTP MODEL

4.1 Methodology

Conjoint analysis is a survey-based statistical technique that breaks down a fan's purchase decision into component parts. We use a Hierarchical Bayesian Multinomial Logit (HB-MNL) model to recover individual utilities. Willingness-to-Pay (WTP) is derived by dividing the utility of an attribute by the negative of the price coefficient:

WTP = β_attribute / |β_price|

4.2 Tier-Specific Fan Segments

Big Six Tier Segments (N=300): Includes "Tourist/Global Fan" (highest WTP, low sensitivity) and "Local Season Ticket" (high sensitivity). Base Price Coef: -0.025 to -0.055.

Mid-Table Tier Segments (N=300): Dominated by "Local Loyal" and "Family" segments. Base Price Coef: -0.065 to -0.095.

Smaller Club Tier Segments (N=300): Characterized by high "Die-Hard Local" presence with very high price sensitivity. Base Price Coef: -0.075 to -0.110.

SECTION 5 — MATCH DATA GENERATION

The system simulates 760 matches (two seasons: 2022-23 and 2023-24) using a sequential round-robin schedule.

5.1 Sequential Standing Simulation

Matches are not pre-categorized. The generator processes Matchweek 1, calculates results, updates the league table, and then processes Matchweek 2. This ensures that the match_stakes_score is always based on the actual standing of the clubs at that specific moment in time.

5.2 EPL-Specific Features

  • TV Slot Encoding: Captures broadcast selection impact (Saturday 12:30 premium vs Saturday 15:00 blackout).
  • European Midweek Fatigue: Models the 7-12% demand penalty experienced by clubs playing UEFA matches midweek.
  • Manager New Bonus: An 8% demand surge applied for 3 months after a new head coach appointment.
  • Away Distance: Models the travel friction for the 3,000 away supporters.

5.3 Booking Curve Archetypes

Each match is assigned one of five logistic demand curves based on club quality and stakes:

  • Immediate Sellout: (t_mid=5, k=0.40) - Matches like ARS vs MCI. 50% sold in 5 days.
  • Early Surge: (t_mid=35, k=0.15) - High stakes but non-Big Six.
  • Consistent Gradual: (t_mid=45, k=0.10) - Standard mid-table fixtures.
  • Late Surge: (t_mid=52, k=0.18) - Relegation battles where fans buy once survival is at stake.
  • Flat/Slow: (t_mid=48, k=0.06) - Low-stakes matches between smaller clubs.

SECTION 7 — DEMAND FORECASTING ENGINE

7.1 Two-Layer Hybrid Model

The forecasting engine uses a hybrid architecture:

  • Layer 1 (Time-Series): STL Decomposition (Period=19) extracts seasonal trends per club. SARIMA (Period=38) models league-wide residuals.
  • Layer 2 (Tabular): A LightGBM Regressor ingests 21 features, including the dynamic stakes score and selling velocity.

SECTION 8 — LP OPTIMIZER

8.1 Objective Function

The LP optimizes for maximum revenue across 30 binary decision variables (10 price points per zone × 3 zones). It linearizes the revenue calculation:

Revenue = Σ (Price_i × Quantity_i × X_i)

8.2 Zone Elasticities

We use industry-standard price elasticity of demand (ε) values:

  • Hospitality: -0.10 (Highly inelastic)
  • Lower Tier: -0.55 (Balanced)
  • General Sale: -1.10 (Highly elastic/price sensitive)

8.3 Operational Constraints

  • Monotonicity: Hospitality price must be ≥ Lower Tier ≥ Upper Tier ≥ General.
  • Away Cap: The away zone is excluded from the objective and fixed at £30.
  • Attendance Floor: Rest-of-League clubs (non-Big Six) must maintain a 65% dynamic attendance floor to protect brand atmosphere.

8.4 Tier Routing Logic

Big Six Route: Revenue-focused. Assumes high base demand; no attendance floor applied.

Rest of League Route: Attendance-protected. Uses promotional pricing if necessary to hit the 65% floor.

SECTION 10 — KNOWN LIMITATIONS

  • Synthetic Signal Consistency: Since both survey data and match attendance are generated from the same synthetic distributions, the model accuracy may be optimistic compared to noisy real-world ticketing data.
  • Broadcast Selection: Real TV slots are chosen by broadcasters based on complex commercial factors (e.g., Sky Sports vs. TNT Sports) not fully modeled in the 12:30/17:30 logic.
  • VRAM/Compute: The NUTS sampler used for Conjoint recovery is computationally expensive and requires approximately 2-5 minutes per tier for convergence.
